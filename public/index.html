Vision · Booking channel

An Agent Booking Channel for Independent Hotels

More travelers are starting to ask AI assistants to plan trips. Today those assistants mostly send people to the same large booking sites that hotels already depend on. We think independent hotels deserve a direct path into this new channel, one where the hotel stays in control, the guest gets a clear offer, and the hotel pays only when a booking actually happens.

This post describes where SwarmForge is heading. It is a vision for a future channel, not a feature you can switch on today.

The problem

Independent hotels live with three pressures at once.

Commission pressure

Online travel agencies bring real demand, but every booking they deliver carries a commission. For a small property, that cost adds up across a season and leaves less room for service, staff and upkeep.

Limited GM time

The GM is often also the revenue manager, the marketer and the person fixing the front desk printer. Building promotions and pushing them to channels rarely gets the time it deserves.

A new kind of visitor

Personal AI agents are beginning to search and compare on behalf of travelers. If hotels are not reachable by those agents in a way they control, the agents will simply route through whoever is.

None of these go away on their own. What can change is how easily a small hotel can put a good offer in front of travelers and their agents, without adding hours of work or another intermediary that takes a cut of every stay.

How it works

1Hotel AI suggestsOffers for soft dates
2GM approvesPrice, rooms, blackout dates
3Offer publishedOpen feed, free to post
4Agent or guest requestsWithin the offer rules
5Booking confirmedFee only on booking

1. The hotel AI spots an opportunity. SwarmForge already helps GMs look at their operations. In this vision, it also notices soft dates, such as a quiet midweek stretch, and drafts an offer: a room type, a price, what is included and how long the offer is valid.

2. The GM approves. Nothing goes live without the GM. They can accept the draft, change the price or inclusions, set how many rooms are available and mark blackout dates where the offer does not apply.

3. The offer is published to a public feed. Approved offers appear on a simple page on Prefertrip.com, one card per offer, and in a machine-readable file that AI agents can read. Posting an offer is free.

4. A guest or an agent requests the stay. A traveler can click the card, or a registered AI agent can send a request for its traveler. Requests must fit the offer: valid dates, available rooms, within the expiry.

5. The booking is confirmed. At first, every request goes to the GM for a one click approval. Then a payment link goes to the guest. Once the guest pays, both sides get a confirmation and the hotel pays a fee for that booking. If nobody books, the hotel pays nothing.

What the GM controls

The point of this channel is to put more control in the hotel's hands, not less. Every offer carries rules that the hotel sets and that software enforces.

  • Price envelope: the published price, plus the lowest price the hotel would ever accept if negotiation is turned on later. Anything outside that range is refused or sent to the GM.
  • Room counts: how many rooms the offer can sell. When they are gone, the offer stops taking requests.
  • Blackout dates: dates the offer does not cover, such as festivals, weddings or dates already full. This removes most of the back and forth before it starts.
  • Expiry: when the offer ends, so stale deals do not linger.
  • Approvals: during the pilot, every booking request needs the GM's approval. Over time a hotel could choose to auto approve requests that fit its rules, but anything that costs money stays with a person.

What the guest or agent experiences

For a traveler browsing on their own, the experience is simple. They see a card with a hotel photo, the hotel name with a link to its website, the dates, the room type, the price, what is included and when the offer expires. One button lets them request the stay. They fill in a short form with their name, email, phone, dates and number of guests.

After the GM approves, the guest receives a payment link from Prefertrip, processed through Razorpay. The link has a short expiry. When payment goes through, the guest and the hotel each receive a confirmation email, and the GM records the booking in their property management system as they would any direct booking. If the link expires unpaid, the rooms go back on offer.

For an AI agent, the steps are the same, just structured. The agent reads the offer file, checks the rules, and sends a request with the traveler's details through a registered gateway. Unregistered bots can read the public offers but cannot place requests. We describe how agents get registered and trusted in our post on the trust layer.

Illustrative example, not real data

A quiet Tuesday to Thursday

Imagine a 20 room boutique hotel with a quiet stretch next month. SwarmForge suggests a two night midweek offer on a deluxe room with breakfast and late checkout. The GM sets the price at ₹6,000 per night, allows 4 rooms, blacks out one date that clashes with a local event, and sets the offer to expire in ten days.

A traveler's AI assistant finds the offer and sends a request for two nights. The GM approves it from their phone. The guest pays ₹12,000 through the payment link and both sides get a confirmation. The hotel pays a single booking fee for that stay. Three of the four rooms remain on offer until the expiry.

The numbers above are made up to show the flow. Actual prices, fees and results depend on each hotel and are not a forecast.

Trust and safety

  • People approve money. No price, refund or booking that costs money happens without a person saying yes.
  • SwarmForge never holds guest funds. Payments are collected through Prefertrip's payment provider, not held by the software.
  • Rules are enforced by software, not guessed by AI. AI can draft and suggest. Whether a request fits the hotel's rules is checked by plain, predictable code.
  • Only registered agents can book. Anyone can read public offers, but placing a request requires an agent that has been identified and approved.
  • Everything is recorded. Requests, approvals, payments and confirmations leave a clear trail the hotel can review.

Why now

Personal AI agents are moving from answering questions to taking actions. Large companies are publishing early standards for how agents should shop and pay. Hotels that are machine readable and rule driven will be easier for those agents to work with. Starting small now, with one hotel and a handful of offers, lets us learn what guests, agents and GMs actually need before the channel grows.

What it isn't

  • It is not a replacement for OTAs. Most hotels will keep using them. This is an additional direct channel.
  • It is not a promise of lower costs in every case. Whether it saves money depends on how many bookings it brings and the fee for each one.
  • It is not an instant booking engine on day one. Without a live connection to the hotel's systems, requests are confirmed by the GM.
  • It is not a payment service. SwarmForge does not hold or move guest money.

Not during the pilot. Every request comes to the GM first.

Support for the stay stays with the hotel, just like a direct booking.

Vision · Trust layer

The Web Needs a Trust Layer for Personal AI Agents

Websites were built for people: browse a page, compare a few options, fill in a form. Personal AI agents are a new kind of visitor. They are software acting for a person, carrying an intent such as "find a room that fits my dates and budget." For that to turn into a real booking, a hotel needs to know who is asking, what the traveler actually allowed, and whether the request fits the hotel's own rules.

This post describes how SwarmForge thinks about that trust layer. It is a design direction, not a network that is already built.

The problem

Some website owners now control which automated systems can read their content. But reading content and making a purchase are very different things. A crawler that indexes a page is harmless compared with an agent that wants to reserve a room and take a payment.

Today a hotel has two poor choices. Block automated traffic and miss the travelers whose agents are trying to book, or let it in and hope nothing goes wrong. Neither works for a small property with no security team. What is missing is a simple, predictable way to welcome trusted agents while keeping everything else at arm's length.

Three separate questions

Who is calling?

The agent and the organization responsible for it.

What did the person allow?

The dates, preferences, maximum price, and whether the agent may book or must ask first.

What does the hotel allow?

Availability, policies, price limits and approval rules.

Keeping these questions separate makes decisions easier to understand and to challenge. A registered agent should not automatically be allowed to spend a traveler's money. A traveler's approval should not override a hotel's inventory or rules. And a hotel's rules should never be bent because an agent is persuasive.

How it works

1ApplyAgent and operator identify themselves
2VerifyCredentials checked
3ApprovedClear limits apply
4Act within rulesRequests checked every time
5MonitoredAccess can be revoked

Discovery is open. Hotels publish their approved offers, availability windows and terms in a public, machine readable form. Any agent can read them, the same way any person can read a website.

Booking is registered. To send a booking request, an agent and the company behind it register first. They identify themselves, agree to the rules of the channel and receive credentials. Those credentials can be checked on every request and withdrawn at any time.

Every request is checked against rules. When a registered agent asks for a stay, software checks the request against the offer: dates, room counts, blackout dates, price limits and expiry. If it fits, it moves forward. If not, it is refused or sent to a person. The AI does not decide whether the rules are met.

Abuse is limited. Request limits and monitoring keep a misbehaving agent from flooding a hotel. Problem agents can be suspended without affecting anyone else.

Negotiation within limits

Later on, an agent may want to ask for a better rate, an extra night or breakfast included. We see negotiation as useful only if the hotel sets the boundaries in advance. The hotel defines a range: the lowest price it would accept and which extras it is willing to add. Within that range, an offer can be made automatically. Outside it, the request goes to the GM or is politely declined.

The principle is simple: AI may propose, but rules decide. A clever message from an agent cannot push a hotel below its floor, because the floor is enforced by plain software rather than by a language model's judgment.

1Agent asksRate or extras
2Rules checkWithin hotel limits?
3Offer or reviewAuto offer, or GM decides
4Traveler approvesExact terms shown

What the GM controls

  • Which offers are public and for how long.
  • Price envelopes: the published price and the lowest acceptable price if negotiation is enabled.
  • Room counts and blackout dates for each offer.
  • Approval rules: which requests need a person. During a pilot, all of them do.
  • Which agents to accept, with the option to decline any registered agent.

What the traveler and agent experience

The traveler sets the mandate: dates, budget, preferences and whether their agent may finalize or must check back. The agent searches participating hotels, sends a structured request and receives a clear answer: an offer with exact terms, a request under review, or a refusal with a reason. Before anything is paid, the traveler sees the final terms and approves them.

A booking is confirmed only when three things have happened: the traveler has given the consent required, the hotel has committed the room, and the agreed payment step has succeeded. If anything fails along the way, the request keeps a clear status so everyone knows where it stands.

Illustrative example, not real data

An agent asks for a better rate

A hotel publishes a weekend offer at ₹7,000 per night and sets a floor of ₹6,300, with breakfast as an optional extra. A registered agent asks for ₹6,000 with breakfast. The rules check says ₹6,000 is below the floor, so the system counters at ₹6,300 with breakfast. The traveler approves, and the request goes to the GM for final confirmation.

A second, unregistered bot tries to send the same request. It can still read the offer, but its booking request is refused because it has no valid credentials.

These numbers are invented to explain the flow and do not describe any real hotel.

A shared record

Important events are recorded: the request, any counter offer, approvals, payment, confirmation, changes and cancellations. The hotel, the agent and the traveler can each understand what happened. Reputation may later help judge which agents are reliable, but it should be explainable, open to appeal, and kept separate from verified identity.

Trust and safety

  • Identity, traveler permission, hotel rules and the transaction record are kept separate.
  • Credentials can be withdrawn at any time.
  • Rules are checked by software every time, not by AI judgment.
  • People approve anything that costs money.
  • SwarmForge never holds guest funds.

Why now

Agent identity and commerce standards are taking shape, including Google's Universal Commerce Protocol and Visa's Trusted Agent Protocol. Rather than invent a proprietary system that every agent must adopt, we intend to connect through standards and adapters as they mature. Designing the trust layer now means hotels are ready when agents arrive.

What it isn't

  • It is not a single universal registry for the whole web.
  • It is not a promise that agent bookings will always be cheaper or better than other channels.
  • It is not a way for agents to bypass a hotel's rules or staff.
  • It is not a reputation score that replaces verified identity.

FAQ

Can any bot book my rooms?

No. Anyone can read public offers, but only registered agents can send booking requests, and those requests must fit your rules.

What happens if an agent misbehaves?

Its access can be limited or withdrawn without affecting other agents.

Can an agent talk my price down?

Only within the range you set in advance. Software enforces the floor.

Does AI decide whether a request is allowed?

No. AI can suggest, but whether a request fits your rules is checked by predictable software.

Will this work with other agent standards?

That is the intention. We plan to connect through emerging standards rather than require one proprietary system.

Is this available now?

No. This is a design direction we are piloting step by step.

Vision · AI desk

A More Capable AI Desk for Independent Hotels

Independent hotels have no shortage of data. Reservations, guest feedback, operating costs, staffing, local events and daily decisions all affect revenue and guest experience. The hard part is bringing those signals together in time to act, often with a small team already doing several jobs at once.

SwarmForge's vision is an AI desk that helps owners and GMs see what matters, decide what to do and follow through, with the right controls. This post describes that direction. It is not a claim that every capability described is available today.

The problem

Scattered signals

Bookings sit in one system, reviews in another, costs in a spreadsheet. Seeing the full picture takes time most GMs do not have.

Too many hats

In a small hotel the GM handles revenue, marketing, staffing and guest issues. Good ideas get lost in the day.

Tools that only talk

Many AI tools answer questions in a chat window and stop there. The work of acting on the answer still lands on the team.

At the same time, the way guests find hotels is changing. Commission based channels remain important, and AI agents are starting to search on travelers' behalf. A small team needs help to keep up without giving away control.

From signals to action

1NoticeA change worth attention
2UnderstandContext and why it matters
3RecommendA practical next step
4ActApproved work gets done
5LearnDid it help?

Notice. The desk brings forward a change or opportunity that deserves attention, such as a run of soft nights, a cost creeping up or a pattern in guest feedback.

Understand. It shows the relevant hotel context and explains why the issue matters, so the GM is not asked to trust a black box.

Recommend. It proposes a practical next step with the assumptions and expected effect visible. The GM can adjust any part of it.

Act. Approved work is carried out, or routed to the person responsible. The desk does not quietly change prices or commit rooms beyond the rules the hotel has chosen.

Learn. What happened is recorded, so the team can judge whether the action helped and do better next time.

One desk, many focused agents

We see a future where a GM gives the desk a goal, such as improving weekday occupancy or reducing avoidable operating costs, and focused AI agents help with the analysis and follow up. One might look at booking patterns, another at local events, another at drafting an offer. They work from hotel approved information, share a clear view of the task and return results in one place for the GM to review.

The goal is not a collection of disconnected bots. It is one coherent desk: what was noticed, what is recommended, what needs approval and what happened afterward, all connected.

What the GM controls

  • Goals: what the desk should focus on this week or season.
  • Boundaries: price ranges, room limits and blackout dates the desk must respect.
  • Approvals: which actions need a person. Anything affecting guests, staff, inventory or money stays with the team.
  • Data: which information the desk may use. The hotel's data stays the hotel's.
  • Scope: which capabilities are turned on at all.

What the team experiences

Instead of hunting through reports, the GM opens the desk and sees a short list of things that matter today, each with a clear explanation and a suggested next step. Approving, editing or dismissing takes a moment. Staff see the tasks routed to them. Later, the GM can look back and see what the desk suggested, what the team decided and how it turned out.

Illustrative example, not real data

Turning soft nights into an offer

Imagine the desk notices that occupancy for the second week of next month is well below the same period last year, while a nearby conference is drawing visitors. It summarizes the booking pace and the event, and suggests a three night package with airport pickup at ₹5,500 per night.

The GM lowers the room count to 5, adds breakfast and approves. The offer is published through the hotel's chosen channels, including the agent booking channel when it is available. Two weeks later the desk reports how many rooms the offer filled, so the GM can decide whether to repeat it.

All figures here are illustrative and do not describe any real hotel or result.

Trust and safety

  • The hotel sets the boundaries, and software enforces them.
  • Every recommendation shows its reasoning and assumptions.
  • People approve anything that affects guests, staff, inventory or money.
  • Actions are recorded so the team can see what happened and why.
  • The desk works only from information the hotel has approved.

Why now

AI is now capable enough to read hotel context and prepare useful options, and guests are beginning to use AI agents to plan travel. Independent hotels that can turn signals into action quickly, without growing their teams, will be better placed for both. Building the desk around clear rules and approvals from the start means new capabilities can be added safely.

Just as important, the desk should know when to stay quiet. A short list of well explained suggestions is more useful to a busy GM than a constant stream of alerts. Part of the design work is deciding what deserves attention, and letting each hotel tune that to its own rhythm.

A foundation that adapts

Hotels will use new channels as expectations and technology change. Some will want help with direct booking, others with reputation, guest communication or connections to their existing systems. Our direction is to fit these into the same model: hotel defined rules, clear proposals, human approval and a record of actions.

What it isn't

  • It is not autopilot. The desk prepares and suggests; people decide.
  • It is not a replacement for the hospitality and judgment of the hotel team.
  • It is not measured by how busy the AI looks. Success is better decisions with less friction.
  • It is not a promise of specific results.

FAQ

Will the AI change my prices on its own?

No. It can suggest changes, but anything affecting prices, rooms or money needs your approval and stays within your rules.

Do I need technical staff?

The aim is that a GM can use the desk directly, without a technical team.

What data does it use?

Only information the hotel has approved. The hotel's data stays under its control.

How do I know why it suggested something?

Every recommendation shows the context and assumptions behind it.

Can I turn capabilities on or off?

Yes. Each hotel chooses its own scope.

Is everything described here available now?

No. This is our product vision, and capabilities will arrive step by step.
